Alpha phase

The Alpha phase is the second stage of the delivery lifecycle. It focuses on testing ideas, reducing risk, and deciding what to take forward into Beta.

Alpha is about trying out possible solutions to the problems uncovered in Discovery.

Teams will:

  • explore different approaches
  • build lightweight prototypes
  • test assumptions
  • learn what is technically feasible
  • gather evidence to decide whether to progress to Beta

Alpha is where you experiment, challenge existing approaches, and expect to throw things away.

Features

Your alpha phase will:

  • typically last 6 -16 weeks, but can last longer in some complicated service frameworks
  • include the creation of multiple prototypes to test ideas
  • test concepts with identified groups of users
  • iterate rapidly based on feedback
  • produce an as-is and to-be service blueprint to be iterated on
  • map user journey flows
  • create user personas and feed into user journey maps
  • refine service concepts
  • explore operational or assisted-digital models

You'll identify and test riskiest assumptions. This might include:

  • user behaviour
  • technical feasibility
  • integration challenges
  • policy or legislative constraints

Explore technology and systems. This might include:

  • conducting technical audits
  • trying APIs, data integrations, or prototypes
  • assessing compatibility with legacy systems

Benefits

By the end of Alpha, teams should have:

  • a recommended solution to take into Beta
  • a solution based on validated learning, evidence, and user testing
  • a working prototype that is not production ready but can show what is viable
  • a high-level roadmap for Beta including technical direction, resource needs, and risks
  • clear ways to measure success and metrics informed by what was tested during alpha
  • evidence you have explored, thrown away and iterated on multiple designs all informed by user research

What the GDS Assessment Panel is looking for

Assessors want to see evidence that:

  • The team has participated in experimentation, learning, and risk reduction.
  • You know which solution is most viable.
  • You have explored an offline route so that non-digital users can still access the service.
  • You have tested the riskiest assumptions.
  • You understand technical feasibility.
  • You have evidence that the design is informed by user insight and not lead by only business and technology needs.
  • You have validated prototypes with users.
  • You are ready with a plan to move into Beta.
  • You have a comms plan.
  • You've planned how people will find out the service exists.
  • You have scope of the service.

Artefacts to produce

You should make sure you include:

  • the identified user groups (primary, secondary and tertiary)
  • research plans
  • user interviews and UR play backs with insights
  • user needs logs
  • user personas
  • as-is and to-be user journey maps
  • as-is and to-be service blueprints
  • a problem statement
  • a visual of the entire problem space and where the service sits within it
  • policy behind the service
  • stakeholder maps
  • a robust plan for beta
  • a good number of experimental prototype iterations for both online and offline that link into user research
  • technical feasibility assessment
  • API and integration exploration notes
  • technical spikes
  • legacy system constraints analysis
  • data flow sketches
  • define what the Minimum Viable Product (MVP) should include for Beta
  • draft KPIs and success measures
  • beta performance metric hypotheses
  • initial analytics approach
  • comms plan or toolkit
  • risk and constraints log
  • evidence naming of the service in line with GDS and research
